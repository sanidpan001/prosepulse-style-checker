    Why a passive voice checker matters more than you think

    Passive voice isn't grammatically wrong — but it is rhetorically expensive. "Mistakes were made" tells you nothing about who made them. "The feature was launched" hides the team that did the launching. In business writing, passive voice creates accountability fog. In fiction, it drains scenes of energy. In academic writing, it bloats sentences until the argument suffocates.

    A good passive voice checker does more than flag the problem — it shows you the way out. ProsePulse detects the classic passive pattern (a form of "to be" followed by a past participle, like "was written" or "is being reviewed"), highlights it in rose, and — the part most free tools skip — generates a one-click rewrite that flips the sentence into active voice. "The ball was thrown by John" becomes "John threw the ball." with a single tap. When there's no "by"-phrase naming the actor, ProsePulse still drafts the active version and nudges you to name the doer, because anonymous actions are usually the real problem.

    You don't have to eliminate every passive — sometimes it's the right choice, like when the receiver of the action matters more than the doer ("The patient was discharged at noon"). ProsePulse's density meter keeps you honest instead of dogmatic: it measures passive constructions per 1,000 words against a target of 30 or fewer, so you can see whether passives are a seasoning or the whole dish.

    The adverb finder online writers actually need

    Stephen King famously advised writers to treat adverbs like dandelions: one on your lawn looks pretty, a yard full of them is a weed problem. The trouble is that adverbs feel like precision. "She ran quickly" seems vivid — until you realize "she sprinted" does the same work with half the words and twice the punch. Weak verbs propped up by "-ly" adverbs are the hallmark of first-draft prose.

    ProsePulse's adverb finder online scans for "-ly" words while ignoring the impostors — words like friendly, daily, and only that end in "ly" but aren't adverbs. Each flagged adverb comes with a concrete hint: swap the weak verb + adverb combo for a stronger verb. Over a few sessions, you'll notice your own patterns. Maybe you suddenly realize you quickly overuse really — and then you stop.

    The adverb density meter targets 20 or fewer per 1,000 words. For reference, most published literary fiction lands well under that; blog drafts routinely triple it. Watching the meter drop as you revise is one of the most satisfying feedback loops in writing.

    Weasel words, clichés, and the honesty of your prose

    Weasel words — very, really, just, quite, basically, obviously — are the packing peanuts of language. They fill space without adding meaning. "This is very important" is weaker than "This decides the outcome." When you catch yourself writing clearly or obviously, ask what you're afraid the reader won't see — then show it instead of announcing it.

    Clichés are weasel words' older cousins: phrases so overused they've lost all voltage. At the end of the day, think outside the box, low-hanging fruit — your reader's eyes glaze over them like banner ads. ProsePulse ships with a baked-in library of dozens of the worst offenders and highlights them in violet, so you can replace them with something you actually mean. The cliché target is strict — 5 or fewer per 1,000 words — because even one can make a paragraph feel borrowed.

    Repeated phrases: the tic you can't hear in your own voice

    Every writer has tics. One novelist starts every third paragraph with "In that moment"; one blogger can't stop writing "the fact of the matter is". Because the phrase feels natural to you, your brain skips it on rereads — but your readers notice by the third occurrence. ProsePulse's repeated-phrase detector finds 3-to-5-word sequences that recur across your text, counts the repetitions, and lets you click straight to each occurrence. It's the closest thing to having a brutally honest friend read your draft.

    How to use the style score and density meters

    The style score (0–100) is a quick-read summary, not a grade on your worth as a writer. It starts at 100 and dips as each issue type's density crosses its target. A first draft scoring 62 is perfectly normal — professional copy often lands in the 75–90 range after one revision pass. Use it as a before/after gauge: run your draft, revise with the one-click passive rewrites and hints, run it again, and watch the number climb.

    The density meters are the real diagnostic tool. Each shows your count per 1,000 words next to its target, with a color-coded bar: green when you're under target, amber when you're drifting, red when a category is running hot. Different genres want different profiles — a legal memo tolerates more passives than a thriller — so treat targets as guardrails, not laws.

    Want a fast routine that actually improves your writing? Next, kill or replace every weasel word — be ruthless with very and really.
